The Verse

Anyone can prompt a post in ten seconds. Nobody can fake eight minutes on camera.

Posted Aug 20, 2026 · send me the link and I’ll wire it up here

Anyone can prompt a LinkedIn post in ten seconds. Nobody can fake eight minutes on camera. Think about karaoke for a second. You didn't pick the song, but you know this one, and the track is doing the work. Drums, bass, three backup singers better than you'll ever be. The chorus hits and the room sings it with you. You're kind of crushing this. Then the track cuts out. No drums. No yellow words. Just a mic, a hum off the speaker, and forty people who stopped talking. And there's still a verse left. That silence is a camera. Everybody's got the backing track now. AI wrote the post, tightened the hook, handed over three variations before the coffee got cold. The whole feed is on key. Which is why being on key stopped counting for anything. Samu Kovács runs a B2B YouTube agency across 45+ channels, and he put it better than I would have (via Exit Five): YouTube is the hedge against AI slop. You can't fake expertise on camera. That's the verse. And the verse always sounds the same. It's the pause before you answer, because the true answer has a condition attached. It's the example with a real customer's real mess in it. It's the sentence that goes "we tried that, it didn't work, here's what we missed." You can prompt a take. You can't prompt a scar. Your buyers already voted on which one they want. 78% would rather get your message as video. 9% want it in text (Wyzowl, cited in Visla). Yesterday I called that a clarity gap. It's a proof gap too. Text is where you claim expertise. Video is where you spend it. So this quarter: Put the practitioner on camera, not the brand. The one who has run it forty times, not the one with the title. Answer the questions sales actually gets, in the order they get them. Go long enough that the track has to cut out. Eight minutes has nowhere to hide. Anybody can sing along. The verse is the job. 🟢

The Compounding Hour

The reclaimed hour has two exits — one melts, one compounds.

Posted Aug 4, 2026 · View live post →

AI didn't make you faster. It gave you a decision you're not making. Every marketing leader I talk to got time back this year. A day a week, sometimes more. Reporting that ran itself. Drafts that wrote themselves. The busywork finally lifted. Then the day disappeared. Nobody could tell me where it went. Here's the thing nobody's naming. That reclaimed hour has two exits. One melts. One compounds. The melt is easy. You spend the hour on more of what the machine just took off your plate. More decks. More dashboards. More motion that looks like work. It feels productive. It's gone by Friday and it never pays you back. The compound is harder. You spend the hour on the one thing that keeps working after you stop touching it. A point of view only you can hold. A positioning call your competitor is too scared to make. A relationship that turns into a pipeline. Same hour. Wildly different year. I learned this the expensive way as a VP. We automated a full day out of the team's week. We spent it, on autopilot, doing prettier versions of the work we'd just automated. A year later we had nothing to show for the time. Not because we wasted it obviously. Because we melted it quietly. The hour is the same for everyone now. The gap is what you do with it. Melt it and you stay busy. Compound it and you build something that pays you for years. 🟢

What Your Buyers Want

Buyers voted 8-to-1 for video. Most teams are still shipping words.

"We tried video. It didn't really land." I hear that constantly. Usually from someone who spent real money finding out. Here's what makes it sting: 78% of B2B buyers would rather get your message as a video. Only 9% want it in text. (Wyzowl, cited in Visla's "LinkedIn Video in 2026.") Roughly 8-to-1. Your buyers already voted. So video isn't the thing that failed. The version you made is. Because most B2B video gets built like a brochure that moves. Three ways it dies: The script goes through four rounds of review and comes back committee-safe. Every line that sounded like a person gets sanded off. The wrong human is on camera. You put a title up there instead of the one who talks to customers all day and actually knows what they're afraid of. It's made to look expensive instead of sound honest. Drone shot of the building. Nobody has ever bought software because of a drone shot. None of that is a budget problem. It's a personality problem. A deck lets vague hide behind bullets. A lens doesn't. That's exactly why buyers want it. And here's the part still stopping most teams: they're pricing a crew. That mandate is gone. The work that used to justify a production company — the edit, the captions, the cutdowns, eighteen versions for eighteen placements — is now remote, fast, and largely handed to AI. What was a six-week production is a Tuesday afternoon. Which means the only real cost left is having something worth saying. So here's where video earns its keep, and none of it needs a studio: Sales opens with a Loom. Sixty seconds. Their name, their account, one specific thing you noticed. A hallway conversation you can send. Ads go video-first. Native video pulls roughly double the engagement of text-only posts (Neal Schaffer). Stop running the static version out of habit. Connected TV. You can buy uninterrupted attention in someone's living room, on the biggest screen in the house, with nothing to scroll past. B2B has barely touched it. Customer interviews, published. Not a testimonial with a logo lockup. Their face, their words, the pause before they answer the hard one. That's proof you can't write. The buyers handed you the format. 8-to-1. They're just waiting for a version with a person in it. 🟢

The Refill Reflex

Why the reclaimed hour vanishes — the reflex to refill it.

AI didn't give you time back. It gave you a test you're failing. Every marketing leader I talk to says the same thing. "We got hours back." Then I ask what they did with them. Silence. Or worse — a list of more meetings, more decks, more Slack. Here's what's happening. I call it the refill reflex. The second your calendar clears, your hand reaches for the faucet. You fill the gap with the same low-value work you were drowning in before. It feels productive. It's not. You just moved the busywork one shelf down. I lived this as a VP. We automated reporting and freed up a full day a week across the team. Within a month that day was gone. Not to strategy. To more of the exact reporting we'd just automated, dressed up prettier. The reflex won. Every time. Reclaimed time isn't found time. It's a budget. And budgets get spent on purpose or they get wasted on autopilot. So here's the only question that matters this quarter. When the hour opens up, are you filling it — or are you spending it? One is a reflex. The other is a decision. 🟢

The Short List

The three moves actually worth the reclaimed hour.

"What do I do with the time AI gave me?" Almost everyone answers the same way. Make more content. Wrong answer. More content is the refill reflex with a marketing badge on. It feels like leverage. It's just volume. And volume was never your problem. Here's the short list I'd actually run. Three things. That's it. One brand bet that compounds. A single swing that only works because a human took a risk a machine never would. The thing your competitors call reckless right up until it works. You don't need ten of these a year. You need one, done all the way. Your buyer's words, on Reddit. Not a survey. Not a dashboard. Go find your ideal customer in the threads where they vent, and read their pain in their own language. The phrases they actually type. The way they describe the problem when no one's selling them anything. That's the voice of customer, raw and unedited. Steal it. Your best copy is already written — by them. One hard positioning call. The kind you've been avoiding because it means saying no to a segment, a feature, a founder's pet idea. Positioning doesn't scale until someone decides. That someone is you. That's the list. Fewer things, done for real, instead of more things done on reflex. AI didn't hand you a to-do list. It handed you room to finally do the work that counts. 🟢
